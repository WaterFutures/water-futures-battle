Between 2025 and 2049, the world in which the battle takes place broadly reflects a continuation of historical trends.

The current climate trend continues and Europe experiences an above-average rise in temperatures.
Precipitation in the Netherlands does not decline significantly, and the trajectory aligns with the High Emission, Wet scenario from the KNMI 2025 projections.
SPEI and availability factor of surface water sources evolve accordingly.

Inflation fluctuates around moderate levels, with occasional shocks but generally stable expectations.
Meanwhile, the mechanisms behind inflation expectations and investor demand remain unchanged.

Electricity prices evolve broadly in line with inflation, reflecting a still-transitional energy system where renewables expand but fossil fuels remain part of the mix.
Indeed, the grid's emission factor decreases following the current decarbonisation policy trajectories, with a slowdown observed towards 2050.
Through a strategic partnership and a deal struck during the 2025 Masterplan negotiations, water utilities secure a fixed electricity price pattern for the entire year.
In 2035, these rates undergo renegotiation and flatten slightly as increased energy storage capacity for renewables stabilizes the grid.
At the same time, solar panel costs continue their downward trajectory due to incremental technological improvements and manufacturing scale.

Similarly to the electricity grid, the emission factors of the pipe options in the initial decade see limited changes, as both PVC and steel industries focus on process optimization.
Around 2035, a structural "decarbonization shock" hits the steel sector.
The industry moves aggressively away from coal-fired blast furnaces toward Electric Arc Furnaces (EAF) and green hydrogen reduction.
By the end of this era, steel has successfully decoupled production from high-carbon feedstocks, achieving a massive 65% reduction in embodied emissions.
PVC, hampered by its reliance on fossil-fuel-derived ethylene, manages only a modest 25% reduction through grid greening and incremental recycling.

No significant changes in per-capita demand across residential and non-residential sectors are observed in the first decade (2025–2035).
From 2035 to 2045, modest demand reductions emerge in the residential sector, driven by more efficient appliances, building retrofits, and gradual improvements in housing standards.
Non-residential demand follows a similar downward trend, as commercial and service buildings progressively adopt more efficient systems.
Toward the end of this period, two municipalities (GM0014 and GM0268) complete the deployment of industrial wastewater reuse systems, resulting in a marked reduction in non-residential demand per activity in those locations.

Population grows steadily, driven by sustained migration toward major urban centres, while rural and peripheral municipalities see their populations gradually thin out.
Housing demand keeps pace with population, but the composition of households shifts: a rising share of single-person dwellings means that the number of houses grows faster than raw population figures alone would suggest.
The business landscape expands in step with population, with new establishments appearing at a regular and predictable rate.
Disposable income continues to grow at a steady pace, supported by gradual productivity improvements.

No new municipality is created in this stage and no new potential water source site has been identified for this stage.
The available pipe and pump options under consideration by the water utilities remain unchanged.
Water demand patterns and the lifeline volume (80 L/day/person) have not changed, too.

At the end of the planning stage (2050), the water utilities reconvene to assess the masterplan's outcomes.
Reliability stands at the top of the government's priorities, driven by the substantial investment budget available during this stage.
GHG emissions follow as the second key objective, given that debt levels remain manageable.
Fairness, meanwhile, has largely been absent from the agenda, but early signals suggest that public pressure for more equitable decision-making is beginning to build.

With the heavy investments of this stage now behind them, the water utilities are tightening their budgets: for Stage 2, the national investment budget they intend to spend is 450 million € per year.
This retrenchment may be an early indicator of the broader economic headwinds gathering on the horizon for Europe.
