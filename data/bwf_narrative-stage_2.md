Between 2050 and 2074, Europe and the Netherlands are tested by several converging stressors: deeper and more sustained economic difficulties, the first tangible effects of climate change, and the migration waves these disruptions set in motion.

The high-emission pathway is now unambiguous.
Decades after the Paris Agreement pledges, the Netherlands and much of Europe bear the cumulative consequences.
Temperatures remain elevated, and a new signal emerges: after years of warmth, the precipitation regime begins to shift.
The dominant scenario transitions toward High Emission, Dry.

Inflation becomes structurally higher and more volatile, with occasional spikes driven by energy and supply disruptions.
Meanwhile, the mechanisms behind inflation expectations and investor demand remain unchanged.

Electricity prices increase broadly in line with inflation, reflecting the significant investment required to expand energy infrastructure.
The decarbonisation of the grid plateaus, but in 2070 efforts pick up and a plan to complete the decarbonisation of the grid completely in the next. 
Electricity price patterns undergo several renegotiations until 2070, when the price remains constant year-round due to expanded storage and flexible energy sources.
Technological progress in solar energy is exhausted and solar costs instead track realised inflation, representing a mature technology whose economics are no longer driven by learning-rate effects but by general price levels.

In the early 2050s, the chemical industry achieves a commercial breakthrough in Chemical Recycling (Pyrolysis) and bio-attributed feedstocks.
This allows PVC to bypass the carbon-heavy extraction of virgin natural gas.
In a sudden leap between 2050 and 2055, PVC’s emission factor drops to 40% of its original value.
During this period, steel continues its refinement, reaching an 80% total reduction by 2075.
The focus shifts from "how we make the material" to "how we keep the material in a closed loop," with secondary (recycled) material becoming the primary source for all new water mains.

Residential per-capita demand continues its gradual decline through improvements in building efficiency, smart home technologies, and greywater reuse, without major discontinuities.
A structural shift accelerates in the non-residential sector.
Industrial and productive facilities in municipalities GM0034, GM0344, GM0363, GM0518, and GM0855 implement large-scale wastewater reuse and internal water-recycling systems.
As these technologies become widespread, non-residential demand per activity drops sharply relative to the previous period.
No shift in consumer behaviour alters the daily and seasonal patterns of water demand.

Population growth decelerates markedly as the cumulative effects of earlier trends level off, and economic turbulence suppresses entrepreneurial activity.
Against this backdrop of financial pressure, household formation patterns reverse: people increasingly consolidate living arrangements, with average occupancy per dwelling rising as cohabitation becomes a more common response to difficult conditions.
Disposable income fails to keep pace with inflation, gradually eroding household purchasing power.

No new municipality is created and no new potential water source site has been identified for this stage.
The available pipe and pump options under consideration by the water utilities remain unchanged.
Lifeline volume (80 L/day/person) has not changed, too.

At the end of the planning stage (2075), the water utilities reconvene to assess the masterplan's outcomes.
With economic pressures mounting, consumers and the governament are accepting a modest reduction in service standards.
However, debt management rises to the top of the agenda and, as the decarbonisation agenda reaches its culmination, GHG emissions now carry two to five times the weight assigned during 2025–2049.
The crisis also sharpens sensitivity to fairness: citizens bearing unequal burdens are making their voices heard, and equitable decision-making edges onto the agenda as a secondary — but no longer negligible — priority.

With the worst of the economic turmoil now behind them, the national government and water utilities look ahead with cautious optimism: for Stage 3, a national investment budget of 850 million € per year has been set aside.
The hardships of this era were not without consequence, but advances in technology, a decarbonising energy system, and a society more attuned to fairness and solidarity suggest that humanity may finally be finding its footing; slowly learning to live within the boundaries of the planet it depends on, and with greater care for one another within it.
