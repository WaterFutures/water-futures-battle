Between 2075 and 2099, the broader picture in Europe brightens: macroeconomic stability returns and productivity gains gather pace, while population growth resumes.

The global energy transition is structurally complete, arresting further forcing.
The climate does not reverse but it stabilises at an elevated, drier state, reflecting the inertia of two centuries of industrial emissions.
Variability persists, but the direction of travel is no longer worsening.

Inflation stabilizes at relatively low levels, supported by technological efficiency and mature energy systems. 
Meanwhile, the mechanisms behind inflation expectations and investor demand remain unchanged.

Electricity prices evolve broadly in line with inflation but with significantly lower volatility.
The grid becomes completley neutral and solar costs keeps growing with inflation.
With large-scale energy storage now fully deployed, electricity prices hold steady across the day and year.

As the global energy grid reaches 100% renewables, the "Process" and "Energy" variables of the emission equation effectively drop to zero.
The remaining challenges are addressed through advanced bio-chemistry and carbon-negative solutions.
By the turn of the century, the embodied carbon of a water pipe is no longer a debt to the environment but a neutral variable. 

Residential per-capita demand undergoes its most pronounced decline, driven by stricter regulatory measures in the largest municipalities.
These include tighter efficiency standards, usage limits, and mandatory reuse systems in residential buildings.
Non-residential demand per activity reaches its lowest levels as water reuse infrastructure is extended to three additional municipalities (GM0599, GM0758, GM0772).
By this time, industrial water reuse has become standard practice and most efficiency gains from technological innovation have already been realized.
No shift in consumer behaviour alters the daily and seasonal patterns of water demand.

This period sees population growth resume on a stable and sustained footing, but the spatial logic of the previous century is inverted.
Where in the old days it was defined by urban pull, nowadays is defined by rural and peri-urban push: improvements in transport infrastructure have so dramatically reduced effective travel times that proximity to city centres no longer commands the premium it once did.
Net migration flows now favour the countryside, and smaller municipalities recover.

No new municipality is created and no new potential water source site has been identified for this stage.
The available pipe and pump options under consideration by the water utilities remain unchanged.
Lifeline volume (80 L/day/person) has not changed, too.

At the end of the planning stage (2100), the water utilities reconvene to assess the masterplan's outcomes.
With most interventions now operating at net-zero emissions, attention shifts back to what water systems were always fundamentally about: serving people reliably and serving them equally.
Reliability and fairness rise together to the top of the agenda, no longer in tension with fiscal or environmental constraints.
Debt, while still tracked, is now one point of consideration among many rather than the constraint that shapes all others.

With Stage 3 now complete, this brings the Battle of the Water Futures to an end.
We make no claim to have predicted how the future will unfold — nor does the BWF pretend to be the definitive arbiter of methodological superiority.
The methodologies you developed are the real outcome of this exercise, and the scenarios were designed as a shared terrain on which to test and compare them.
A single scenario, however carefully constructed, can only reveal so much: luck and uncertainty played their part throughout, as they always do in a competition fought on the terrain of the unknown. 
We hope the challenge proved stimulating, and look forward to seeing the BWF continue as a shared framework for comparing and advancing the methods of our field.
